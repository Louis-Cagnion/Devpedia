# Model Distillation

A large language model is very capable, but slow and expensive to run. **Distillation** transfers part of its capabilities to a much smaller, faster, cheaper model, using a teacher/student analogy.

## The principle: a teacher that generates the student's training data

Rather than training the small model (the **student**) only on raw data found on the internet, the large model (the **teacher**) is used to produce the expected answers itself, reasoning included, step by step. These generated answers become the student's training data:

```text
Teacher model (large, slow, expensive)
  --> generates answers along with their reasoning
  --> these answers become training data
Student model (small, fast, cheap)
  --> learns to reproduce the SAME kind of reasoning
```

The student never inherits the teacher's internal weights: it learns a reasoning **pattern** from examples the teacher produced, exactly like a human student learns a method from exercises corrected by their teacher, without ever accessing their actual thinking.

## The result: less capable, but far faster and cheaper

A distilled model never matches its teacher, but stays surprisingly capable relative to its size and running cost. DeepSeek applied this method with its R1 model: the reasoning data generated by R1 was used to train much smaller models (from 1.5 to 70 billion parameters), with better results than if those small models had had to discover that reasoning themselves through [reinforcement learning](/?c=ia&s=fondamentaux-du-deep-learning&p=entrainement-descente-de-gradient).

## The question that stays open: permission, not the technique

Distillation itself has nothing problematic about it: it's a standard machine learning method, and many model providers explicitly allow it in their license. What separates a legitimate use from a contested one is never the technique, but **permission**:

| Situation | Status |
|---|---|
| Distilling your own model into smaller models | Normal, widely documented and allowed |
| Distilling from a third-party model's outputs, without authorization, in violation of its terms of use | Contested: a question of respecting terms of use, not of the technique itself |

> **Pitfall:** judging distillation "acceptable" or not purely on its technical nature. The same method becomes legitimate or not depending on whether the teacher model's owner allows or forbids it in their terms of use.

## 📋 Summary

| | |
|---|---|
| **Key takeaways** | Distillation trains a small model (student) on the answers generated by a large model (teacher), without ever copying its weights. The result is less capable than the teacher but far faster and cheaper to run. |
| **Tools you can use** | No specific tool: distillation describes a training method, usable with any deep learning framework. |
| **Pitfalls to avoid** | Confusing the technical question (how to distill) with the permission question (whether you're allowed to distill from this particular model). |
| **Best practices** | Check the teacher model's license before any distillation from its outputs; distilling your own model stays unambiguous. |
