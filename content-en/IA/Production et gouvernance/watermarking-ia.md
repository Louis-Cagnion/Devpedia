# Watermarking AI-Generated Content

Telling AI-generated content apart from human-produced content becomes a direct concern as models improve: traceability for a company that needs to audit its own outputs, a legal obligation under [EU AI regulation](/?c=ia&s=production-et-gouvernance&p=reglementation-europeenne-ia) (transparency required for any limited-risk content, deepfakes in particular), and the fight against large-scale disinformation. **Watermarking** addresses this need by embedding, in the generated content itself, a signal that allows identifying it after the fact, but the technique differs radically depending on whether text, an image, or audio is being marked.

## Text Watermarking: a Statistical Bias, Not a Hidden Character

There's nothing to hide inside text: unlike an image, there's no spare pixel where an invisible signal could be tucked away. The technique therefore relies on something else: slightly influencing the model's choices during generation itself.

An LLM picks each next token from a [probability distribution over the entire vocabulary](/?c=ia&s=nlp-llm&p=nlp-et-llm): several candidate tokens have a non-zero probability at the same position in the text, and any of them would produce a correct sentence. A secret key, known only to the model provider, makes some of these candidates slightly more likely than others at each generation step:

```text
Probability distribution over the vocabulary, at a given position
      │
      ▼
Secret key -> slightly favors certain candidate tokens
      │
      ▼
Token chosen (the bias remains invisible when reading)
```

Taken in isolation, a single word proves nothing: any human could have made the same choice. Repeated over hundreds or thousands of tokens, however, this slight bias forms a statistical pattern that a detector holding the key can measure, without needing access to the model itself.

> **Pitfall:** looking for a word considered "typical of AI" (like *delve*, often cited as a supposed marker) and treating it as proof of AI generation. There's no secret list of forbidden words: a model's preference for certain words comes from its training, not from a watermarking mechanism, and watermarked text doesn't need to contain any particular word.
>
> **Best practice:** treat this watermarking as probabilistic statistical evidence, never as a binary verdict: a detector returns a confidence score, not a certainty.

### Limitations Specific to Text

The statistical signal is fragile for reasons specific to text, independent of any deliberate attempt to circumvent it:

| Situation | Effect on the signal |
|---|---|
| Very short text (a few sentences) | Too few tokens for a statistical pattern to emerge: detection isn't reliable |
| Rewriting or paraphrasing | Each reworded word is a new choice, independent of the original bias: the signal fades progressively |
| Summarization | A summary is a new generation of tokens, not a copy: the source text's signal isn't carried over |
| Translation | Entirely changes the space of candidate tokens (different language): the signal doesn't survive the switch |

> **Pitfall:** presenting text watermarking as a guarantee against any misuse. A signal as fragile as a statistical bias is only truly reliable on long, unedited text in its original language, yet a large share of real-world uses (partial copy-paste, rewording, translation) already makes it disappear.
>
> **Best practice:** communicate honestly about this limitation rather than presenting text watermarking as a robust solution: it's one more statistical clue, not proof of authenticity in the cryptographic sense.

## Image and Audio Watermarking: a Mark Inserted into the Signal

Unlike text, an image or an audio stream has physical room to hold a signal without altering its perception: a pixel has several possible shades, an audio sample several nearby values, all perceived identically by the human eye or ear.

| Approach | Principle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Imperceptible watermark | A pattern encoded into the pixels or the sampling, invisible/inaudible to a human but readable by a dedicated detector | The audio watermarking mentioned in [Cloning a Voice](/?c=ia&s=voix-ia&p=cloner-une-voix) |
| Perceptible watermark | A mark that is directly visible or audible | An "AI-generated" watermark overlaid on an image |
| Provenance metadata ([C2PA](https://c2pa.org)/*Content Credentials*) | A cryptographically signed metadata chain, attached to the file, that traces every creation/modification step | An image whose metadata lists: generated by a given model, then edited by a given piece of software |

The [C2PA](https://c2pa.org) standard (*Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity*) differs from the first two approaches: it doesn't modify the content itself, it attaches a verifiable history to it. That's exactly where its weakness lies: this metadata disappears with a simple export or a screenshot, without touching the visual/audio content itself, whereas a properly designed imperceptible watermark holds up better.

> **Pitfall:** treating an imperceptible watermark as unbreakable once and for all. Aggressive compression, cropping, or deliberate audio processing can degrade or erase the mark, a point already flagged on the audio side in [Cloning a Voice](/?c=ia&s=voix-ia&p=cloner-une-voix).
>
> **Best practice:** combine several layers (imperceptible watermark and C2PA metadata) rather than relying on just one, each having a different breaking point.

## A Limitation Common to All Techniques: Detecting, Not Preventing

Whether for text, image, or audio, watermarking answers a single question, *after the fact*: was this content generated by AI? It answers no other: it doesn't prevent a model from generating problematic content, blocks nothing at generation time, and is only useful if a detector is actually queried afterward.

> **Pitfall:** presenting watermarking as a security measure that prevents misuse. It's an after-the-fact traceability tool, not a prevention mechanism: marked content can circulate freely, be used for fraud or disinformation, without any mechanism stepping in before the harm is done.
>
> **Best practice:** place watermarking within a broader chain of traceability and accountability (transparency obligations under [EU AI regulation](/?c=ia&s=production-et-gouvernance&p=reglementation-europeenne-ia), usage policies, moderation), never as a sufficient standalone solution.

## Key takeaways

| | |
|---|---|
| **Key takeaways** | Text watermarking statistically biases token choice using a secret key; it loses reliability on text that's short, rewritten, summarized, or translated. Image/audio watermarking embeds an imperceptible signal in the content, or relies on signed provenance metadata (C2PA). In every case, watermarking detects after the fact; it prevents nothing at generation time. |
| **Tools you can use** | A statistical detector holding the secret key, for text. An imperceptible watermark or C2PA metadata, for image and audio. |
| **Pitfalls to avoid** | Confusing a word considered "typical of AI" with proof of watermarking. Presenting a watermark as an infallible guarantee or as a prevention mechanism rather than a detection one. |
| **Best practices** | Treat a detector's result as a probabilistic score, never a clear-cut verdict. Combine several watermarking layers (imperceptible and metadata) rather than just one. Place watermarking within a broader traceability chain, not as a standalone solution. |
